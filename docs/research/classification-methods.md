# Fix retrieval first, then teach locally

> Research of Oct 4, 2026, kept as written; the notes behind it are in [classification-methods/](classification-methods/). It led to steps 6g ([eval/recall.md](../../eval/recall.md)) and 6h ([step-6h.md](../specs/step-6h.md)). Since then the pipeline moved to the `deeper` shortlist: 66.7% exact, not the 54.0% this report starts from.

Our current design, an embedding shortlist of 50 Categories followed by a hosted LLM choice (TypeSafe Jev), is the method the published literature and every large production system converge on. At **54.0% exact leaf accuracy** on 198 Listings it already sits in the same band as the only comparable public figure: about 60% for a single automated method on the full Shopify taxonomy. No published zero-shot, text-only method beats it clearly. Supervised systems that report 85–97% get there with hundreds of thousands to millions of in-domain labels, fewer or narrower classes, or lenient metrics. Those numbers are not targets for us. The evidence points to three levers, in this order. First, raise shortlist recall: a better or fine-tuned retriever, plus kNN over labelled products. Second, describe each Category with a short written definition instead of a bare path (+3 to +9 points in two independent studies). Third, after that, distil the hosted pipeline into a local classifier that runs at embedding speed and sends only low-confidence Listings to Jev. The cheapest and most valuable first step is a bigger eval set. Shopify publishes an Apache-2.0 benchmark of 48k Shopify-labelled products, so we can shrink the ±7-point error bars of our 198-item eval before we chase gains of 3–5 points. Small local LLMs used zero-shot (Laya: 13.1%) are a dead end until they are fine-tuned.

**Which numbers you can read against our 54%.** Only plain top-1 accuracy over a single gold label counts: our own rows, Walmart and PGKD. Even those come from different regimes. Walmart is supervised on 25M labels. PGKD has 335 classes, five times fewer than our 1,862. **Weighted F1** (Rakuten, Amazon, Icecat) comes close to accuracy for single-label tasks, but those systems were trained in-domain, and head classes dominate the metric. **Hierarchical F1** (Superlinked; 0.921 on WDC in a Nova thesis ([Luedecke 2025](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/181471/1/Master_Thesis_FALL25_58411.pdf)); 98.59% on Google taxonomy ([arXiv 2508.20013](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20013v1))) gives partial credit, so exact leaf is always lower. **Acceptance and SME ratings** (Shopify 85%, Elsevier 94.3%) count any defensible answer as correct. One 2025 zero-shot GPT/Claude study reports 63.7–85.8%. It claims 248 categories, but its chance rate (3.21%) implies about 31 options per decision, and the full text was not accessible ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949719125000184)). Treat it as unverified.

## Retrieve-then-choose wins, and the shortlist is the ceiling

Our step from 23.7% (embedding alone) to 54.0% (embedding + Jev) is the textbook result. Amazon, Elsevier, Instacart and IReRa all retrieve candidates and let an LLM pick one. Gains over the retriever alone run **+20 to +33 points** where both are reported. Elsevier found that skipping the label-reduction step cost about 32 points ([COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.14.pdf)), and IReRa lifted RP@10 from 36.8 to 65.8 on one dataset ([arXiv 2401.12178](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.12178)). The same papers explain our 10-vs-50 result. Amazon can use K=10 only because its retriever is fine-tuned on millions of catalogue items. Elsevier, with an off-the-shelf bi-encoder, explored K=10–100 and settled on 40 ([Amazon](https://aclanthology.org/2024.customnlp4u-1.22/)). With bge-small, recall at 10 is the binding constraint, so K=50 helps.

Our depth profile tells the same story: 74.7% top level, 65.7% level 2, 54.0% exact. Supervised systems usually get the top level above 90%. Losing a quarter of Listings at the root suggests the shortlist often misses the right branch altogether. No amount of chooser quality fixes that. We have not measured **recall@K of the gold Category in our shortlist**, and no paper reports it for product data. That makes it the first number to get. If recall@50 is near 70%, Jev already picks correctly from most shortlists that contain the answer, and gains must come from retrieval.

Swapping in a bigger off-the-shelf embedder will not close the gap by itself. On the same taxonomy at the same depth, a 1.5B embedding model reaches only **0.425 strict hierarchical F1**, and small models reach 0.24–0.30 ([Superlinked](https://superlinked.com/docs/examples/taxonomy-classification.md)). Since hF1 gives partial credit, the exact leaf figure is lower still. Moving from bge-small to Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B or EmbeddingGemma-300M probably buys single digits, not 30 points, and costs several times the compute per Listing. Off-the-shelf cross-encoder rerankers added nothing on that benchmark (mxbai-rerank-base-v2 tied retrieval at 0.425). The smaller MS MARCO model scored lower (0.315). These rerankers are trained to judge query→passage relevance, not to match a product to a Category, so they only become interesting once fine-tuned on our own pairs.

## Category definitions and labelled neighbours are the measured cheap wins

The strongest prompt-side evidence concerns how Categories are described, not chain-of-thought. In Amazon's ablation, full descriptive paths beat short names, and adding an **LLM-written definition per Category** was the biggest single gain: +3.8, +4.0 and +3.1 points across three locales. Chain-of-thought added nothing beyond that ([Amazon](https://aclanthology.org/2024.customnlp4u-1.22/)). Elsevier saw the same effect from the other side: removing label descriptions dropped acceptability from 94.3% to 85.7%, and so did removing parent-path context ([COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.14.pdf)). On the embedding side, full paths beat leaf names by 9 hF1 points for a strong model but not for a tiny one ([Superlinked](https://superlinked.com/docs/examples/taxonomy-classification.md)). Writing 1,862 definitions is a one-off job, and we can embed them alongside the path to improve retrieval as well. The catch is cost. Fifty definitions in every Jev prompt roughly double input tokens, so expect the $62 per 1M Listings to rise towards about $100–120. Put definitions into the embedding index first, where they cost nothing at run time.

The best data discovery is **Shopify/product-catalogue** ("The Catalogue"). It holds 48,289 real Shopify-merchant products (38.6k train, 9.66k test) with title, description, image and a full Shopify path, spanning 10,476 categories, under **Apache 2.0** ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Shopify/product-catalogue)). Cut each path to depth 3 and it maps onto our 1,862 Categories. Coverage of the long tail is unmeasured, and the card does not state the taxonomy version. The Catalogue serves three jobs. It is an eval set: 2,000 items shrink the error bars to about ±2 points, and its `potential_product_categories` field gives a ready-made "acceptable alternative" score. It is a kNN index: about 38.6k vectors at 384 dimensions is roughly 60 MB, and neighbours' labels merge into the shortlist. And it is a source of definitions and few-shot examples. Domain shift is real, since these are Shopify merchants rather than Amazon sellers, but kNN and evaluation tolerate it far better than supervised training does.

Two other shortcuts exist, but each applies only to some inputs. Shopify's MIT-licensed taxonomy repo ships a Shopify→Google Product Taxonomy mapping covering every Shopify category. It maps about 2.35 Shopify leaves onto each Google ID, so inverting it gives a candidate set, not an answer. The fan-in shrinks once leaves are cut to depth 3 ([Shopify/product-taxonomy](https://github.com/Shopify/product-taxonomy)). That helps whenever a merchant feed carries `google_product_category`, which Google, Meta and Shopify all accept as an optional override ([Google Merchant Center](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324436)). Amazon Reviews '23 breadcrumbs could be mapped once per unique breadcrumb, but no public mapping exists. Real merchant feeds do not carry Amazon breadcrumbs, and if our 198 labels were researched with the breadcrumb in view, any breadcrumb-based classifier will look better on our eval than it would in production ([Amazon Reviews '23](https://amazon-reviews-2023.github.io/)). Keep a breadcrumb-blind baseline.

## Local LLMs fail zero-shot, while distillation is the route to free inference

Laya's results match the literature exactly. Level-by-level LLM walks lose because each wrong upper-level choice is final. Elsevier's LLM-only traversal scored 50.0%, below even the old supervised model ([COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.14.pdf)). Walmart's top-down hierarchy reached 70% against 92% for flat classification ([arXiv 1903.04254](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.04254)). A small model choosing from a shortlist did worse still (8.1%), which says the problem is knowledge, not structure. Every company that runs a small model in production fine-tunes it first. Shopify runs a fine-tuned 7B VLM, and Instacart runs a LoRA Llama-3-8B distilled from an offline teacher pipeline ([Shopify](https://shopify.engineering/leveraging-multimodal-llms); [Instacart](https://company.instacart.com/tech-innovation/building-the-intent-engine-how-instacart-is-revamping-query-understanding-with-llms)). Constraining Laya's output to the shortlist fixes invalid answers but not its ignorance. A prefill-heavy prompt of about 1,000 tokens on a 16 GB Mac shared by 4–8 workers is also a poor fit for the 50 changes/s peak.

Distillation fits our constraints. PGKD trained a BERT-base student from Claude 3 Sonnet labels and the student beat its teacher on 335 Amazon classes (44.3% vs 41.6%) while running about 130x faster ([arXiv 2411.05045](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05045v1)). Synthetic-data studies show topic-like tasks lose only 2–6 points against real data ([Li et al., EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.07849)). Our measured cost turns that into a plan. Labelling the 1M bulk load with the current pipeline costs **$62** and takes about 3.5 hours at 80 req/s. The labels can then train a logistic-regression or MLP head on frozen bge-small vectors, which adds well under a millisecond and almost no memory on top of today's 9.4 ms embedding. Better still, they can contrastively fine-tune bge-small on (Listing, Category path) pairs, which raises shortlist recall for the existing pipeline. The student's ceiling is roughly the teacher's 54%, and it inherits the teacher's mistakes. The payoff is a confidence gate. Amazon sent only about **20% of traffic** to the LLM and kept its accuracy gains ([Amazon](https://aclanthology.org/2024.customnlp4u-1.22/)). The equivalent for us would cut Jev spend and latency several-fold for confident Listings.

Label noise caps what any method can show. Amazon needed multiple rounds of human review for its eval sets. Multi-purpose products legitimately fit several Categories. In a comparable large-taxonomy task, human experts under time pressure scored only **65–90%** against a senior expert ([COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.14.pdf)). Shopify reached 95% only with an ensemble plus human review of the disagreements ([Toloka](https://toloka.ai/blog/building-shopify-s-product-catalogue-at-ai-speed/)). Shopify also found that some distinctions, such as infant vs toddler apparel, need images, so part of our deep-level error cannot be fixed from text alone.

## Conclusion

The headline finding is that our accuracy problem lives in retrieval, while our budget problem is cost and latency, and the literature offers one remedy for both. Better candidates, from definitions, labelled neighbours and eventually a fine-tuned embedder, lift Jev's ceiling. The labels Jev produces on the first bulk load are what make that fine-tuning, and a local confidence gate, affordable. A realistic text-only target is the 60–70% band, which is an inference from the literature, not a published figure. Reaching 85–90% would take images or large-scale supervised training of the kind Shopify, Amazon and Walmart run.

The other takeaway is about measurement. Every candidate improvement in the ranked list is worth 3–9 points, which is smaller than our current error bars. Until a larger eval exists, most A/B results on the 198 items cannot be distinguished from noise. Shopify's own labelled benchmark makes fixing that a one-day job.
